<div class="section-label">Published</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">
    <a href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/741696">Reach and Retrenchment of the Environmental State: Global Climate Politics in the Amazon Rainforest</a>
  </div>
  <div class="paper-venue">
    <em>American Journal of Sociology</em>, online first
    <span class="award">
      ASA Environmental Sociology Graduate Student Paper Award<br>
      ASA Sociology of Development Graduate Student Paper Award<br>
      ASA Political Sociology Graduate Student Paper Award
    </span>
  </div>
  <div class="paper-abstract">
    How do states build and lose the capacity to decarbonize? Drawing on a comparative-historical analysis of Brazil's drastic reduction and later resurgence of CO₂ emissions from 1985 to 2022, this article describes climate change as an emerging state-building domain with global and scientific dimensions. It uncovers a new pathway for decarbonization: domestic bureaucrats and national scientists build state capacity by leveraging transnational ties that provide shelter from politics and resources for policy experimentation. Democratic backsliding triggers the collapse of that minimal agreement, and opponents erode the foundations of state capacity by targeting the global and scientific basis of decarbonization.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Whose Merit, which Redistribution? Elites, Taxes, and Transfers in Brazil and South Africa</div>
  <div class="paper-coauthors">with Graziella Moraes Silva and Matias Lopez</div>
  <div class="paper-venue">Forthcoming, <em>Social Forces</em></div>
  <div class="paper-abstract">
    Scholarship finds that elites credit their own success to hard work and attribute poverty to laziness. We argue that these two components of merit constitute different dimensions, with varied implications for redistributive policies and different salience depending on national contexts. Using elite survey data from Brazil and South Africa, we examine whose merit matters for which types of redistribution—finding that elite perceptions of merit and redistributive preferences are contingent on national repertoires of evaluation.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Qualitative Methods and the Digital Era: How Computation Entangles Fieldwork</div>
  <div class="paper-venue">Under review</div>
  <div class="paper-links">
    <a href="https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/tqvm7_v1">preprint</a>
  </div>
  <div class="paper-abstract">
    This paper argues that computation transforms qualitative fieldwork in quieter ways than machine learning—through scraping, parsing, optical character recognition, archiving, and zooming. I show how these tools entangle fieldwork spatially, temporally, and epistemically, drawing on my own comparative-historical study of Brazil's decarbonization capacities conducted within a PhD structured by COVID-19.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">An Autopsy of State Finance: How Transnational Legal Structures Insulate Policies from Politics</div>
  <div class="paper-venue">Under review</div>
  <div class="paper-abstract">
    This article shows how transnational legal structures of state finance enable some policy funds to survive periods of state dismantling. Drawing on environmental finance data, 70 in-depth interviews, and archival research in Brazil since 1990, it demonstrates how lawyers and bureaucrats insulate policy by distributing funding, governance, and disbursement rules across public–private and national–international jurisdictions.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Putting Power into Boxes: Challenges and Advantages of Conducting Surveys with Elites</div>
  <div class="paper-coauthors">with Graziella Moraes Silva, Matias Lopez, and Debora Thome</div>
  <div class="paper-venue">Under review</div>
  <div class="paper-abstract">
    Drawing on metadata from over 300 elite samples across more than 60 countries, we reassess three recurrent critiques of elite surveys: that probabilistic sampling is ill-suited for elites, that elites are uniformly hard to survey, and that standardized instruments fail to capture elite attitudes. We show that structured questionnaires can discipline elite respondents by predefining analytical categories and making variation within elite worldviews empirically visible.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Easy and Hard Decarbonization: The (Im)possibility of Ecological Modernization in the Global South</div>
  <div class="paper-venue">Writing up</div>
  <div class="paper-abstract">
    Building on the distinction between easy and hard redistribution, I propose a parallel distinction for decarbonization. Expansions of the environmental state occur when the trade-off between greening and growth is small enough to generate supporting policy coalitions (easy decarbonization). When the trade-off hardens, business elites block CO₂ reduction (hard decarbonization). I argue that Brazil and many Global South countries are currently in a phase of hard protection.
  </div>
</div>
